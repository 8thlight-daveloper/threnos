# Why Threnos

A threnos is a lament. Shakespeare's poem
["The Phoenix and the Turtle"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Phoenix_and_the_Turtle) (1601) ends with one, sung
over two birds that burn together. The name reads three ways, and we mean all three.

## The phoenix rises

In mythology, the phoenix burns and a new bird rises from the ashes. This project builds software that way, where each
component is deleted and built again from its contracts. The new code is not the old code, but it meets the same
requirements and acceptance criteria unless the contracts change.

## The turtledove stays true

The turtle in the title is a turtledove, a symbol of constancy. Here the dove is the contracts. They say what correct
means, and they outlive every rebuild. A rebuilt component is checked against them, and one that drifts fails
acceptance.

## The poem refuses the story

Shakespeare gives the phoenix no rebirth. It is one of a kind and dies "leaving no posterity." The two birds become one,
and the poem buries truth and beauty with them.

We read that as the loss of what people know about their code. When a person writes every line, truth is knowing what
the code does and why, and beauty is the craft in how it is written. People and agents now write the same code, and no
one can say who wrote what. Fewer people hold knowledge of a system's inner workings, and a rebuild does not bring it
back. Only the contracts keep what correct means.

This project does not keep an understanding of the code; in that sense, it buries truth and beauty too. The name mourns
that. Truth in software has moved up before, from machine code to higher-level languages. Now natural language is canon,
and it removes the constraints that made code a puzzle. We loved those small problems, and the work of making something
complex read as simple to anyone. We keep the hope of the myth beside the grief of the poem, and we do not claim the
poem shares our hope.

The last stanzas of the poem are the threnos itself:

```text
Beauty, truth, and rarity.
Grace in all simplicity,
Here enclos'd in cinders lie.

Death is now the phoenix' nest;
And the turtle's loyal breast
To eternity doth rest,

Leaving no posterity:—
'Twas not their infirmity,
It was married chastity.

Truth may seem, but cannot be:
Beauty brag, but 'tis not she;
Truth and beauty buried be.

To this urn let those repair
That are either true or fair;
For these dead birds sigh a prayer.
```

Read the lament as one line of lowercase letters.
